## Conventions
<!-- key: conventions -->

- Vennin & Starobinsky (arXiv:1506.04732), Pattison et al. (arXiv:1707.00537) and Ezquiaga et al. (arXiv:1912.05399) use the reduced Planck mass $M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}$, the logarithm of the scale factor as time, counted forward, a prime for a derivative with respect to the field, and the reduced potential $v=\frac{V}{24\pi^{2}M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{4}}\approx\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 2], also in `formalism.md#stochastic-inflation`. The stochastic variable $\mathcal{N}$ is the number of e-folds realised from a field value until the end of inflation, so the field value is the argument and $\mathcal{N}$ the "time" of the first-passage problem; this is neither of the two e-fold directions of the skill's conventions block (`formalism.md#conventions`) but a random duration.
- Pattison et al. and Ezquiaga et al. work with the coarse-grained curvature perturbation $\zeta_{\rm cg}$, the deviation of $\mathcal{N}$ from its mean (Eq. 13 below); Vennin & Starobinsky work with the moments of $\mathcal{N}$ and their derivatives with respect to $\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle$ (Eq. 21 below).
- Ezquiaga et al. call $\mathcal{L}_{\rm FP}^{\dagger}$ the adjoint Fokker–Planck operator (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Sect. 2.1, text after Eq. 8]; Calderón-Figueroa & Seery call the same equation the backward Kolmogorov equation, "or "adjoint" Fokker–Planck equation", write the reduced Planck mass $M_{\mathrm{P}}$, the transition probability $\bm{\mathsf{P}}$, the survival probability $\bm{\mathsf{S}}$, the first-passage density $\bm{\mathsf{Q}}$ and the transition duration $N_{\star}=N-N_{0}$ (arXiv:2510.04707) [src: Sect. 2.1.2; Notation and conventions].
- Blachier & Ringeval write their results in the variables $\chi_{0}$ and $\hat{\alpha}$ of their Sect. 2 and the lifetime $\Delta N_{0}$ (arXiv:2511.21388) [src: Sect. 4.1]; their Sect. 2 was not read, so their variables are named here and not converted.
- Itô interpretation. Vennin & Starobinsky write the Fokker–Planck equation "In the Itô interpretation", and state that "keeping terms explicitly depending on α exceeds the accuracy of the stochastic approach in its leading approximation (1)", α labelling the family between Itô (α = 0) and Stratonovich (α = 1/2) (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 2, footnote 5]. <!-- num: not a value -->

## The derivation
<!-- key: the-derivation -->

### Step 1 — the stochastic process and its conditions
<!-- key: step-1-the-stochastic-process-and-its-conditions -->

The coarse-grained field contains "the modes k for which $k\lesssim\sigma aH$", with a cutoff "satisfying $e^{-1/(3\epsilon_{1})}\ll\sigma\ll 1$"; "Under this condition, the physical results are independent of σ" (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 1, footnote 2]. The Langevin equation of `formalism.md#stochastic-inflation` "is valid at leading order in slow roll", and its noise is "a white Gaussian noise" with $\left\langle\xi\left(N\right)\right\rangle=0$ and $\left\langle\xi\left(N\right)\xi\left(N^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle=\delta\left(N-N^{\prime}\right)$ [src: Sect. 1, inline, text after Eq. 1]. The e-fold number is the time variable because "any other time variable defined through a or H is a stochastic quantity, and cannot be used to label the Langevin equation, otherwise one would describe a physically different process" [src: Sect. 2].

The forward Fokker–Planck equation for the field distribution, in the Itô interpretation, is $\frac{\partial}{\partial N}P\left(\phi,N\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\left[\frac{V^{\prime}}{3H^{2}}P\left(\phi,N\right)\right]+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}\left[\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}P\left(\phi,N\right)\right]\,.$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 4]. The authors "never use the "volume weighted" variant" of it, "since then the resulting distribution is not normalizable" [src: Sect. 2, footnote 6].

Beyond one field and beyond slow roll, Ezquiaga et al. write the Langevin system as $\mathrm{d}\bm{\Phi}_{\rm cg}/\mathrm{d}N=\bm{F}_{\rm cl}+\bm{\xi}$ (their Eq. 5) with the noise correlator $\left\langle\xi_{i}\left(\bm{x}_{i},N_{i}\right)\xi_{j}\left(\bm{x}_{j},N_{j}\right)\right\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln k_{\sigma}}{\mathrm{d}N}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi_{i},\Phi_{j}}\left[k_{\sigma}(N_{i}),N_{i}\right]\delta(N_{i}-N_{j}).$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 6], and note that "we are implicitly working in the gauge where time is unperturbed, i.e. the uniform N-gauge in the present case. The power spectra need therefore to be computed in that gauge" [src: Sect. 2.1, text after Eq. 6]. Pattison et al. 2019 explain that "in general, there is a gauge correction to the amplitude of the stochastic noise", because the amplitude "is usually calculated in the spatially-flat gauge, while the number of e-folds is used as the time variable"; the corrections "vanish in the slow-roll limit", and in the cases they compute, ultra-slow roll and the Starobinsky model among them, they "find the corrections to be negligible in practice" (arXiv:1905.06300) [src: abstract].

### Step 2 — from the e-fold distribution to curvature correlators (δN)
<!-- key: step-2-from-the-e-fold-distribution-to -->

In the uniform-density gauge the curvature perturbation is the local excess of expansion, $\zeta(\bm{x})=N\left(t,\bm{x}\right)-N_{0}(t)\equiv\delta N\,,$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 13], evaluated in the separate-universe approach, in which on large scales "the local density, expansion rate, etc., obey the same evolution equations as a homogeneous FLRW universe" [src: Sect. 3.1, text after Eq. 13]. The standard calculation assumes in addition that the field follows its classical equation after Hubble crossing, so that the dispersion of N "only comes from the field dispersion at Hubble crossing $\delta\phi_{*}$"; "In most cases, this is a good approximation", with two caveats: the ratio of quantum kick to classical drift "is not the correct way to assess the importance of stochastic effects", and the first slow-roll parameter can become "tiny or even vanishes in some transient phase" (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 3.1, text after Eqs. 17–18]. Stochastic δN drops that assumption: N is replaced by the random number $\mathcal{N}$ of e-folds realised between $\phi_{*}(k)$ and $\phi_{\rm end}$ [src: Sect. 3.2]. ζ and δN "need not be small" [src: Sect. 3.1, last paragraph].

Because $\mathcal{N}$ receives contributions from all modes crossing the Hubble radius between the two points, its variance $\delta\mathcal{N}^{2}\left(k\right)\equiv\left\langle\mathcal{N}^{2}\left(k\right)\right\rangle-\left\langle\mathcal{N}\left(k\right)\right\rangle^{2}\,.$ [src: Eq. 19] gives the power spectrum as $\mathcal{P}_{\zeta}\left(k\right)=\mathcal{P}_{\delta\mathcal{N}}\left(k\right)=\left.\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\delta\mathcal{N}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\left\langle\mathcal{N}\right\rangle}\right|_{\left\langle\mathcal{N}\right\rangle=\ln\left(k_{\mathrm{end}}/k\right)}\,.$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 21], using that the mean e-fold number equals the logarithm of the ratio of end and crossing wavenumbers up to "slow-roll corrections that we do not need to take into account at leading order in slow roll" (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 3.2, text after Eq. 20]. The local non-Gaussianity follows from the third moment, $f_{\mathrm{NL}}=\frac{5}{72}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\delta\mathcal{N}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\delta\mathcal{N}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle}\right)^{-2}\,,$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 23], "where 5/72 is a conventional historical factor" (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 3.2, text after Eq. 23]. Pattison et al. print the same relation as $f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{\mathrm{local}}=\frac{5}{72}\frac{\left\langle\delta N_{\mathrm{cg}}^{3}\right\rangle}{\mathrm{d}\left\langle\mathcal{N}\right\rangle^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\left\langle\delta N_{\mathrm{cg}}^{2}\right\rangle}{\mathrm{d}\left\langle\mathcal{N}\right\rangle}\right)^{-2}\,.$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 15], as printed: the numerator lacks the second derivative that the sentence before it states for the bispectrum (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 2.1.2, text before Eq. 15] and that Eq. 23 of arXiv:1506.04732 carries.

The PDF itself is that of the coarse-grained perturbation, $\delta N_{\mathrm{cg}}\left(\bm{x}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{x}\right)-\left\langle\mathcal{N}\right\rangle=\zeta_{\mathrm{cg}}\left(\bm{x}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}}\int_{k_{*}}^{k_{\mathrm{end}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\zeta_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\,,$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 13], which integrates all modes from $k_{*}$ to $k_{\rm end}$ (see "What goes wrong" for the difference with a single Fourier mode).

### Step 3 — first-passage moments: the adjoint (backward) equations
<!-- key: step-3-first-passage-moments-the-adjoint -->

Vennin & Starobinsky apply first-passage-time analysis through the Itô lemma (their Eqs. 24–25). The initial condition is part of the result: "This calculation therefore relies on a specific choice of initial (in fact, pre-inflationary) conditions, since all trajectories emerge from $\phi_{*}$ at initial time. In principle, other choices could be made, even if most physical quantities (in particular, perturbations during the last 60 e-folds) do not depend on them" (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 3.2, footnote 7].

- **Which end is reached.** With two absorbing points $\phi_{1}<\phi_{*}<\phi_{2}$ (or the reverse), a function h with $h^{\prime\prime}\left(\varphi\right)v\left(\varphi\right)=h^{\prime}\left(\varphi\right)\frac{v^{\prime}\left(\varphi\right)}{v\left(\varphi\right)}\,,$ [src: Eq. 27] gives the probability to end at $\phi_{1}$, $p_{1}=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\phi_{*}}^{\phi_{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{v\left(x\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}x}{\displaystyle\int_{\phi_{1}}^{\phi_{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{v\left(x\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}x}\,,$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 29]. If the potential is maximal at $\phi_{2}$ and monotonous on an interval reaching it, the denominator diverges as $\phi_{2}\to\infty$ and $p_{2}=0$: "if $\phi_{2}$ is sufficiently large, its precise value plays no role, since inflation always terminates at $\phi_{1}=\phi_{\mathrm{end}}$" [src: Sect. 3.3.1, text after Eq. 29].
- **Mean.** The function f of Eq. 30 of arXiv:1506.04732 (`formalism.md#stochastic-inflation`), with "boundary conditions $f\left(\phi_{1}\right)=f\left(\phi_{2}\right)=0$" [src: Sect. 3.3.2, inline, text after Eq. 30], turns the Itô lemma into $\mathcal{N}=f\left(\phi_{*}\right)+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{N}}f^{\prime}\left[\varphi\left(N\right)\right]\sqrt{2v\left[\varphi\left(N\right)\right]}M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}\xi\left(N\right)\mathrm{d}N\,.$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 31], whose average is the function f evaluated at the starting point (their Eq. 32). That the stochastic integral averages to zero is "non-trivial", "since both the integrand and the upper bound are stochastic quantities", "but it can be shown rigorously" [src: Sect. 3.3.2, footnote 10]. The integration constant $\bar{\phi}$ of Eq. 33 has "no generic expression", but lies between $\phi_{1}$ and $\phi_{2}$, equals $\phi_{\rm max}$ for a potential symmetric about a maximum near which inflation proceeds, and equals $\phi_{2}$ when $\phi_{2}\to\infty$ in the right-panel case of their Fig. 1; "In practice, almost all cases boil down to one of the two previous ones" [src: Sect. 3.3.2, text after Eq. 33].
- **Classical limit and the classicality criterion.** A saddle-point evaluation of the inner integral gives $\left.\left\langle\mathcal{N}\right\rangle\right|_{\mathrm{cl}}=\int_{\phi_{\mathrm{end}}}^{\phi_{*}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}\frac{v(x)}{v^{\prime}(x)}\,,$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 35]: "The classical trajectory thus appears as a saddle-point limit of the mean stochastic trajectory" [src: Sect. 3.3.2, text after Eq. 35]. Requiring the second-order term of the expansion of 1/v to be small defines $\eta_{\mathrm{cl}}=\left|2v-\frac{v^{\prime\prime}v^{2}}{{v^{\prime}}^{2}}\right|\,.$ [src: Eq. 36], and, checked against their Fig. 3, "the classical trajectory is a good approximation to the mean stochastic one if and only if $\eta_{\mathrm{cl}}\ll 1$" [src: Sect. 3.3.2, text after Eq. 36]. The saddle-point step assumes a monotonous potential: "Strictly speaking, this is only true if the potential is a monotonous function of the field, but this is most often the case in the part of the potential that is relevant to the inflationary phase" [src: Sect. 3.3.2, footnote 13].
- **Variance.** With $g^{\prime\prime}\left(\phi\right)v\left(\phi\right)-g^{\prime}\left(\phi\right)\frac{v^{\prime}\left(\phi\right)}{v\left(\phi\right)}=-2{f^{\prime}}^{2}\left(\phi\right)v\left(\phi\right)\,,$ [src: Eq. 39] and $g(\phi_{1})=g(\phi_{2})=0$, $\delta\mathcal{N}^{2}=g(\phi_{*})$ [src: Sect. 3.3.3, text after Eq. 40]; its leading classical limit is $\left.\delta\mathcal{N}^{2}\right|_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{2}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{4}}\int_{\phi_{\mathrm{end}}}^{\phi_{*}}\mathrm{d}x\frac{v^{4}\left(x\right)}{{v^{\prime}}^{3}\left(x\right)}\,.$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 43].
  - `[der]` Through Eq. 21 this leading term returns the classical spectrum given in `formalism.md#stochastic-inflation`.
- **All central moments.** $\sigma_{p}=\delta\mathcal{N}^{p}$ solves $\sigma_{p}^{\prime\prime}-\sigma_{p}^{\prime}\frac{v^{\prime}}{v^{2}}=-2pf^{\prime}\sigma_{p-1}^{\prime}-p\left(p-1\right){f^{\prime}}^{2}\sigma_{p-2}$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Eq. 48] satisfying $\sigma_{p}\left(\phi_{1}\right)=\sigma_{p}\left(\phi_{2}\right)=0$ (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 3.3.4, inline, text after Eq. 48], with $\sigma_{0}=1$ and $\sigma_{1}=0$ by convention.

Pattison et al. write the same hierarchy for the raw moments $f_{n}=\langle\mathcal{N}^{n}\rangle$, now with a reflective wall instead of a second absorbing point: $f_{n}^{\prime\prime}-\frac{v^{\prime}}{v^{2}}f_{n}^{\prime}=-\frac{n}{vM_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}f_{n-1}\,.$ [src: Eq. 17] with $f_{0}=1$ and $f_{n}(\phi_{\text{end}})=0\,,\quad f_{n}^{\prime}(\phi_{\text{uv}})=0\,.$ [src: Eq. 18], solved by $f_{n}(\phi)=n\int_{\phi_{\text{end}}}^{\phi}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}\int_{x}^{\phi_{\text{uv}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}e^{\frac{1}{v(y)}-\frac{1}{v(x)}}\frac{f_{n-1}(y)}{v(y)}\,,$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 19]. This "relies on performing integrals of increasing dimension, which quickly becomes numerically heavy but provides a convenient way to study the first few moments" [src: Sect. 2.2, text after Eq. 19]. The wall at $\phi_{\rm uv}$ "can be necessary to renormalise infinities appearing in the theory", and the results "are still independent of $\phi_{\text{uv}}$ and of the exact nature of the wall, reflective or absorbing, provided $\phi_{\text{uv}}$ lies in some range" [src: Sect. 2.1.2, text after Eq. 12].

### Step 4 — the full PDF: characteristic function and heat equation
<!-- key: step-4-the-full-pdf-characteristic-function-and -->

Moments do not give the tail. Pattison et al. resum the hierarchy into the characteristic function $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}(t,\phi)\equiv\left\langle e^{it\mathcal{N}(\phi)}\right\rangle\,,$ [src: Eq. 20], which obeys, at each t, the ordinary differential equation $\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}-\frac{v^{\prime}}{v^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}+\frac{it}{vM_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}\right)\chi_{\mathcal{N}}(t,\phi)=0\,.$ [src: Eq. 21] with $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}\left(t,\phi_{\text{end}}\right)=1\,,\quad\frac{\partial\chi_{\mathcal{N}}}{\partial\phi}\left(t,\phi_{\text{uv}}\right)=0\,.$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 22]. The PDF of the coarse-grained curvature perturbation is the inverse Fourier transform $P\left(\zeta_{\mathrm{cg}},\phi\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}e^{-it\left[\zeta_{\mathrm{cg}}+f_{1}\left(\phi\right)\right]}\chi_{\mathcal{N}}\left(t,\phi\right)\mathrm{d}t\,,$ [src: Eq. 25]. For a quadratic potential Eq. 21 has an exact solution in Kummer functions, with the wall sent to infinity (their Eq. 27), and the numerically inverted PDF "has a larger mean value, a larger spread and seems to be less Gaussian" as v grows [src: Sect. 2.3, Fig. 2 caption].

Equivalently, the PDF obeys a single partial differential equation in $(\mathcal{N},\phi)$, $\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}-\frac{v^{\prime}}{v^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}-\frac{1}{vM_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathcal{N}}\right)P\left(\mathcal{N},\phi\right)=0\,.$ [src: Eq. 28], with $P\left(\mathcal{N},\phi_{\text{end}}\right)=\delta(\mathcal{N})\,,\quad\frac{\partial P}{\partial\phi}\left(\mathcal{N},\phi_{\text{uv}}\right)=0\,.$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 30]. The authors warn that Eq. 28 "does not have the structure of a Fokker-Planck equation, and should in any case not be confused with the usual Fokker-Planck equation considered in stochastic inflation which governs the PDF of the field value" [src: Sect. 2.4, text after Eq. 28]; that forward equation reads, in their notation, $\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}\left[vp(N,\phi)\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\left[\frac{v^{\prime}}{v}p(N,\phi)\right]-\frac{1}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial N}p(N,\phi)=0\,.$ [src: Sect. 2.4, footnote 2, Eq. 29]. With $u(\phi)=\int_{\phi_{\text{end}}}^{\phi}e^{-\frac{1}{v(\tilde{\phi})}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\phi}}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}\,,$ [src: Eq. 31] it becomes $\left(ve^{-\frac{2}{v}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathcal{N}}\right)P(\mathcal{N},u)=0.$ [src: Eq. 32], "a heat equation for a one dimensional medium with diffusivity $ve^{-2/v}$", but with boundary conditions where Eq. 30 "involves distributions of times at fixed spatial positions", so standard heat-equation solvers "would need to be adapted" [src: Sect. 2.4, text after Eq. 32].

Ezquiaga et al. state the general structure: the PDF of $\mathcal{N}$ "can then be shown to obey the adjoint Fokker-Planck equation" $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}}P_{{\bm{\Phi}}}\left(\mathcal{N}\right)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FP}}^{\dagger}\left({\bm{\Phi}}\right)\cdot P_{{\bm{\Phi}}}\left(\mathcal{N}\right),$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 8], with, when all fields are in slow roll, $\frac{1}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FP}}^{\dagger}=-\sum_{i}\frac{v_{\phi_{i}}}{v}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{i}}+v\sum_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi_{i}^{2}}\,,$ [src: Eq. 9], the boundary condition $P_{\bm\Phi}(\mathcal{N})=\delta(\mathcal{N})$ on the end-of-inflation surface $\epsilon_{1}=1$, and a reflective condition on a surface $\mathcal{C}_{\rm uv}$ [src: Sect. 2.1, text after Eq. 10]. In Sect. 2 they "remain generic and do not assume slow roll"; Sect. 3 treats "single-field slow-roll models" with Eq. 9 [src: Sect. 2.1, text after Eq. 10]. They stress that the field PDF and $P_{\bm\Phi}(\mathcal{N})$ "obey very similar equations" but "are conceptually very different objects" [src: Sect. 2.1, text before Eq. 12].

### Step 5 — why the tail is exponential: the poles of the characteristic function
<!-- key: step-5-why-the-tail-is-exponential-the-poles-of -->

Ezquiaga et al. compare two forms. The classical, Gaussian picture gives $\left.P_{{\bm{\Phi}}}(\mathcal{N})\right|_{\mathrm{cl}}\underset{\mathcal{N}\gg 1}{\propto}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathcal{N}^{2}}{\int_{\bar{k}}^{k_{\mathrm{end}}}\mathcal{P}_{\zeta,\mathrm{cl}}(k)\mathrm{d}\ln k}\right]\,,$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 13]; perturbative $f_{\rm NL}$, $g_{\rm NL}$ corrections modify it "with polynomial corrections in $\mathcal{N}$", "which cannot capture the exponential decay" of the expansion $P_{{\bm{\Phi}}}(\mathcal{N})=\sum_{n}a_{n}({\bm{\Phi}})e^{-\Lambda_{n}\,\mathcal{N}}\,,$ [src: Eq. 12; Sect. 2.1, text after Eq. 13]. The argument for Eq. 12:

1. Inserting the inverse transform into Eq. 8, Ezquiaga et al. obtain $\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FP}}^{\dagger}\left({\bm{\Phi}}\right)+it\right]\chi_{\mathcal{N}}\left(t,{\bm{\Phi}}\right)=0$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 16], with $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}(t,{\bm{\Phi}})=1$ on $\mathcal{C}_{\rm end}$ and a vanishing normal derivative on $\mathcal{C}_{\rm uv}$ [src: Sect. 2.2, text after Eq. 16].
2. The characteristic function is expanded as $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}\left(t,{\bm{\Phi}}\right)=\sum_{n}\frac{a_{n}({\bm{\Phi}})}{\Lambda_{n}-it}+g(t,{\bm{\Phi}})\,,$ [src: Eq. 18], g regular. Justification, as the paper gives it: "since the PDF $P_{{\bm{\Phi}}}(\mathcal{N})$ vanishes for negative $\mathcal{N}$, according to Titchmarsh's theorem, its Fourier transform, i.e. the characteristic function, is a holomorphic function in the upper half-plane"; reality of the PDF gives $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}^{*}(t,{\bm{\Phi}})=\chi_{\mathcal{N}}(-t^{*},{\bm{\Phi}})$, so "all poles lie on the negative imaginary axis at locations $t=-i\Lambda_{n}$"; and because Eq. 16 is linear in t, "the poles in t only appear when enforcing the boundary conditions. This explains why the $\Lambda_{n}$ do not depend on ${\bm{\Phi}}$, but only on the location of the surfaces $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{end}}$ and $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{uv}}$" (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Sect. 2.2, text after Eq. 18]. Footnote 2 records that the argument "in a previous version of the paper was incomplete"; v3, read here, carries the corrected one [src: Sect. 2.2, footnote 2].
3. Closing the contour in the lower half-plane, "assuming that g ... does not increase exponentially or faster at large |t|", gives $P_{\bm{\Phi}}(\mathcal{N})=\sum_{n}\,a_{n}({\bm{\Phi}})\,e^{-\Lambda_{n}\,\mathcal{N}}\,.$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 19], with residues $a_{n}(\phi)=-i\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\chi_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}\left(t=-i\Lambda_{n},\phi\right)\right]^{-1}\,.$ [src: Eq. 20]. "This form is always valid, but at large $\mathcal{N}$, only the first terms in the sum dominate, and it provides a tail expansion in terms of decaying exponentials"; "while the amplitude of the tail, controlled by $a_{0}$, depends on the initial field value, the decay rates $\Lambda_{n}$ are universal for a given potential" [src: Sect. 2.2, text after Eqs. 19–20].
4. **Equivalent eigenvalue problem.** $\mathcal{L}_{\rm FP}^{\dagger}$ is self-adjoint for the weighted product $\langle F_{1},F_{2}\rangle_{w}=\int\mathrm{d}{\bm{\Phi}}F_{1}({\bm{\Phi}})F_{2}({\bm{\Phi}})w({\bm{\Phi}})\,,\quad\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\quad w({\bm{\Phi}})=\frac{e^{1/v({\bm{\Phi}})}}{v({\bm{\Phi}})}\,,$ [src: Eq. 22], so its eigenvalues are real; with $\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{FP}}\cdot\Psi_{n}\left({\bm{\Phi}}\right)=-\Lambda_{n}\Psi_{n}\left({\bm{\Phi}}\right)\,,$ [src: Eq. 23], $\Psi_{n}=0$ on $\mathcal{C}_{\rm end}$ and a vanishing normal derivative on $\mathcal{C}_{\rm uv}$, the PDF is $P_{\bm{\Phi}}\left(\mathcal{N}\right)=\sum_{n}\alpha_{n}\Psi_{n}\left({\bm{\Phi}}\right)e^{-\Lambda_{n}\mathcal{N}}\,.$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 25]. This "also shows that the characteristic function does not possess multiple pole", otherwise terms $\mathcal{N}^{k-1}e^{-\Lambda_{n}\mathcal{N}}$ would appear [src: Sect. 2.3, text after Eq. 25]. The weighted scalar product is acknowledged to colleagues in footnote 3 [src: Sect. 2.3, footnote 3].

`[der]` The exponential tail therefore rests on three things the papers state: a number of e-folds that is never negative (holomorphy in the upper half-plane), a discrete set of poles fixed by the boundaries (a bounded field domain between $\mathcal{C}_{\rm end}$ and $\mathcal{C}_{\rm uv}$), and a regular part g without exponential growth. Blachier & Ringeval add the converse for an unbounded potential: "For unbounded potentials, the poles of the characteristic function are not forming a discrete set as they do in the bounded case. As such, one cannot find pure exponential decaying behaviour for the associated probability density distribution" (arXiv:2511.21388) [src: Sect. 4.1, footnote 6].

### Step 6 — worked cases: flat well and constant slope
<!-- key: step-6-worked-cases-flat-well-and-constant-slope -->

**Flat quantum well** (Pattison et al.). For $v=v_{0}$ on a width $\Delta\phi_{\rm well}$ above $\phi_{\rm end}$, with a reflective wall at $\phi_{\rm end}+\Delta\phi_{\rm well}$, $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}\left(t,\phi\right)=\frac{\cosh\left[\alpha\sqrt{t}\mu\left(x-1\right)\right]}{\cosh\left(\alpha\sqrt{t}\mu\right)}\,.$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 50], with $x\equiv(\phi-\phi_{\mathrm{end}})/\Delta\phi_{\text{well}}$, $\alpha\equiv(i-1)/\sqrt{2}$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 4.1, inline, text after Eq. 50] and $\mu^{2}=\frac{\Delta\phi_{\text{well}}^{2}}{v_{0}M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}^{2}}$ [src: Eq. 51], "the only combination through which these two quantities appear" [src: Sect. 4.1, text after Eq. 51]. Two exact expansions follow. Term-by-term inversion gives $P\left(\mathcal{N},\phi\right)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\mu}{\mathcal{N}^{3/2}}\times\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\left[2(n+1)-x\right]e^{-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\mathcal{N}}\left[2(n+1)-x\right]^{2}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\left[2n+x\right]e^{-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\mathcal{N}}\left[2n+x\right]^{2}}\right\}\,.$ [src: Eq. 52]; the heat-equation route, with coefficients fixed by matching Eq. 52 at $\mathcal{N}\to 0$, gives $P\left(\mathcal{N},\phi\right)=\frac{2\pi}{\mu^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{\pi^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\mathcal{N}\right]\sin\left[x\pi\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]\,,$ [src: Eq. 59], resummed as $P\left(\mathcal{N},\phi\right)=-\frac{\pi}{2\mu^{2}}\vartheta_{2}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}x,e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\mathcal{N}}\right)\,,$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 60]. Eq. 52 is an expansion on the "left" tail, near zero e-folds, and Eq. 59 on the "right" tail, at large e-fold numbers; "in the limit where all terms in the sums are included, both expressions match exactly for all values of $\mathcal{N}$" (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 4.2, second comment after Eq. 60]. The tail is set by the n = 0 mode, $P(\zeta_{\mathrm{cg}},\phi)\propto e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\mu^{2}}\zeta_{\mathrm{cg}}}\,,$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 62]: the PDF "decays exponentially as $e^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{cg}}}$, i.e. much slower than the Gaussian decay $e^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{cg}}^{2}}$", and "the decay rate of the distribution is independent of $\phi$" (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 4.2, fifth comment after Eq. 60]. Ezquiaga et al. recover the same rates as poles, $\Lambda_{n}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\,.$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 33], "real, positive and independent of $\phi$" [src: Sect. 3.1.1, text after Eq. 33].

**Constant slope** (Ezquiaga et al.). For $v\left(\phi\right)=v_{0}\left(1+\alpha\frac{\phi}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}\right),$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 41] (here α is the slope, not the constant of Eq. 50 of arXiv:1707.00537), in the "almost-constant" regime $\phi_{\rm uv}\ll M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}/\alpha$ the poles solve $\tan\left(\sqrt{\Lambda_{n}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4v_{0}}}\mu\right)=-2\frac{v_{0}}{\alpha}\frac{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}{\phi_{\text{uv}}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{n}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4v_{0}}}\mu\,.$ [src: Eq. 45]; the root z = 0 is discarded because the characteristic function "is in fact regular, and does not possess a pole" there [src: Sect. 3.2.1, text after Eq. 45]. In the wide-well limit $\frac{\phi_{\text{uv}}}{M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}\gg\frac{v_{0}}{\alpha}\,.$ [src: Eq. 47], $\Lambda_{n}^{\mathrm{wide}}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4v_{0}}+\frac{\left(n+1\right)^{2}\pi^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\left(1-4\frac{v_{0}M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}{\alpha\phi_{\text{uv}}}+\cdots\right)$ [src: Eq. 46], and "in the wide-well regime, adding a small slope to the potential is enough to highly suppress the tails" [src: Sect. 3.2.1, text after Eq. 47]; in the narrow-well limit $\Lambda_{n}^{\mathrm{narrow}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\left[\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{\alpha\phi_{\text{uv}}}{\pi^{2}v_{0}M_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{Pl}}}}+\cdots\right],$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 49], which "in fact corresponds to the flat-potential limit" [src: Sect. 3.2.1, text after Eq. 50].

**The tail exists in the classical regime too.** With $\mathcal{P}_{\zeta,\mathrm{cl}}\simeq 2v_{0}/\alpha^{2}$ and $\Lambda_{0}=\alpha^{2}/(4v_{0})$ in the wide well, $P_{\phi}^{\mathrm{wide}}(\mathcal{N})\underset{\mathcal{N}\gg 1}{\propto}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathcal{N}}{\mathcal{P}_{\zeta,\mathrm{cl}}}\right)\,.$ (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Eq. 51], against the Gaussian $\left.P_{\phi}(\mathcal{N})\right|_{\mathrm{cl}}\underset{\mathcal{N}\gg 1}{\propto}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathcal{N}^{2}}{\mathcal{P}_{\zeta,\mathrm{cl}}N_{\mathrm{cl}}}\right)\,,$ [src: Eq. 52]; "as soon as $\mathcal{N}\gg N_{\mathrm{cl}}$, the amount of power on the tail is greatly enhanced in the full stochastic theory compared to the classical, Gaussian approximation", and the two "coincide for $\mathcal{N}=N_{\mathrm{cl}}$" [src: Sect. 3.2.1, "Comparison with the classical result"]. The abstract states the scope of the conclusion: the exponential tails "are inevitable, and do not require any non-minimal feature as they simply result from the quantum diffusion of the inflaton field along its potential", applied to "a few relevant single-field, slow-roll inflationary potentials" (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: abstract].

### Step 7 — the classical limit seen from the characteristic function
<!-- key: step-7-the-classical-limit-seen-from-the -->

Expanding Eq. 21 in v is "equivalent to an expansion in the diffusion term" (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 3.1]. At leading order the PDF of $\delta N_{\rm cg}$ is a Dirac distribution: "one simply shuts down quantum diffusion" [src: Sect. 3.1.1, text after Eq. 35]. At next-to-leading order $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{NLO}}}(t,\phi)=\exp\left[itf_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{NLO}}}\left(\phi\right)-\gamma_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{NLO}}}vt^{2}\right]\,,$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 38], a Gaussian in t, hence a Gaussian PDF [src: Sect. 3.1.2, text after Eq. 40]. The expansion is valid only with the wall sent to infinity, because "the second boundary condition in Eq. (22) cannot be satisfied simultaneously with the first condition" [src: Sect. 3.1.1, footnote 3]. `[der]` The classical expansion, which drops the wall and truncates in powers of t, thus describes the neighbourhood of the maximum, as Eq. 13 of arXiv:1912.05399 does, and not the poles of Step 5.

## Variants and what they cost
<!-- key: variants-and-what-they-cost -->

- **Moment hierarchy (Vennin & Starobinsky; Pattison et al. Eq. 19).** Closed-form nested integrals for each moment and the spectrum and $f_{\rm NL}$ through Eqs. 21 and 23; cost grows with the dimension of the integrals (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 2.2, text after Eq. 19]. Gives no tail.
- **Characteristic function (Pattison et al. Sect. 2.3).** One ODE per value of t, then a numerical inverse Fourier transform; exact solutions for a quadratic potential and a flat well (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eqs. 27, 50].
- **Heat equation (Pattison et al. Sect. 2.4).** One PDE for $P(\mathcal{N},\phi)$; combined with the characteristic function it gave the closed form Eq. 60: "the results from both methods ... have been necessary to derive this closed form" (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 4.2, first comment after Eq. 60].
- **Poles and residues, or eigenvalues (Ezquiaga et al.).** Tail expansion to any order from the zeros of $\chi_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}$, or from the spectrum of $\mathcal{L}_{\rm FP}^{\dagger}$ with the boundary conditions of Eq. 23; "solving one problem automatically gives the solution for the other" (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Sect. 2.3, text before Eq. 26]. The formal solution behind the eigenvalue route needs a time-independent operator: Calderón-Figueroa & Seery note that "strong time dependence invalidates the formal solution (21) which plays a critical role in the spectral method" (arXiv:2510.04707) [src: Sect. 5.4].
- **Survival probability (Calderón-Figueroa & Seery).** The first-passage density is $\bm{\mathsf{Q}}(\phi_{\text{end}},N\;|\;\phi_{0},N_{0})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial N}\bm{\mathsf{S}}(N\;|\;\phi_{0},N_{0})\;.$ (arXiv:2510.04707) [src: Eq. 14], with S solving the backward Kolmogorov equation and vanishing at $\phi_{\rm end}$ (their Eqs. 15–16a); the upper boundary $\phi_{\rm uv}$ "is not physical, but if $\phi_{\text{{uv}}}$ is taken sufficiently large it will not influence the result" [src: Sect. 2.1.2, text after Eq. 16a]. They print the backward operator as $-\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathsf{P}}}{\mathrm{d}N_{0}}=-\frac{V^{\prime}}{3H}\frac{\partial\bm{\mathsf{P}}}{\partial\phi_{0}}+\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{\mathsf{P}}}{\partial\phi_{0}^{2}}=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{P}}.$ [src: Eq. 12], as printed: the drift has $3H$ where their Langevin equation, $\mathrm{d}\phi=-\frac{V^{\prime}}{3H^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}N+\frac{H}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,,$ [src: Eq. 7], has $3H^{2}$; Eq. 15 repeats the $3H$.
- **Instanton (Calderón-Figueroa & Seery).** A saddle point of the Martin–Siggia–Rose path integral for the restricted transition probability, related to the Schwinger–Keldysh influence functional; the authors "argue that our framework has a number of advantages", and that "it reliably captures the tail behaviour in cases where existing techniques do not apply, including cases where the noise amplitude has strong time dependence", and extends "beyond the slow-roll limit, and to models with multiple fields" (arXiv:2510.04707) [src: abstract]. For the linear potential of Ezquiaga et al. it reproduces the tail to leading order in $N_{\star}$, $\ln\bm{\mathsf{Q}}(N_{\star})\approx-\frac{1}{2}\frac{N_{\star}}{\mathcal{P}_{\zeta}}+\Or(1)\quad\text{for $N_{\star}\gg 1$.}$ [src: Eq. 69]; whether the $\Or(1)$ corrections matter more than the uncomputed prefactor is unclear, "This issue deserves further investigation", and they are omitted "to avoid giving a misleading result" [src: Sect. 3.3, text after Eq. 69]. The method gives $\bm{\mathsf{Q}}$ "without ever needing to specify the relation between the field $\phi$ and the curvature perturbation $\zeta$" [src: Sect. 3.3, text after Eq. 69]. Cost: only the leading exponential, without the prefactor.
- **Spectral method on a hilltop (Tomberg 2026).** Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Fokker–Planck operator for a quadratic hilltop, with trajectories allowed to cross the hilltop and "get stuck near a reflecting boundary on the other side"; the "well-known exponential tail first flattens out and then forms a peak near a maximal ΔN value" (arXiv:2606.09690) [src: abstract].
- **Beyond slow roll, numerically (Figueroa et al.).** In ultra-slow roll, the coupled evolution of the coarse-grained background and of the modes "leads to a non-Markovian process from which we determine the highly non-Gaussian tail" (arXiv:2012.06551) [src: abstract].
- **Time-reversed stochastic inflation (Blachier & Ringeval).** Conditions on the lifetime first and uses δN at given lifetime; on a flat semi-infinite potential with constant drift the forward result is $\chi_{{}_{0}}^{2}e^{-\hat{\alpha}}P\negthinspace\left(\zeta_{\mathrm{fw}}|\phi_{{}_{0}}\right)\simeq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\dfrac{\zeta_{\mathrm{fw}}}{\chi_{{}_{0}}^{2}}\right)^{-3/2}e^{-\hat{\alpha}^{2}\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{fw}}}{2\chi_{{}_{0}}^{2}}},$ (arXiv:2511.21388) [src: Eq. 50], "a decaying exponential multiplied by a power-law", whose exponential is "identical to the one of the tilted quantum-well in the so-called "wide" limit"; the time-reversed result has the index "−5/2" instead of "−3/2" and "the exponential decay rate has a factor of two difference" [src: Sect. 4.1, text after Eq. 50]. "Taking average and marginalising over lifetimes do not commute" [src: Sect. 4.1]; the authors add that "it is very well possible that the differences between the time-reversed and the forward formalism would become negligible in a classical background", but "we do not have solutions of time-reversed stochastic inflation in non-flat, or even bounded, potentials" [src: Sect. 4.1, last paragraph].
- **Gradient interactions.** Higher-dimensional Langevin equations reveal a "pullback" effect that damps the tails of the first-passage-time distributions (arXiv:2509.05124), summarised in `results.md#stochastic-inflation-and-the-tails`.

## What goes wrong
<!-- key: what-goes-wrong -->

- **Truncated perturbative expansions on the tail.** Gaussian or $f_{\rm NL}$/$g_{\rm NL}$-corrected PDFs give only polynomial corrections to Eq. 13 of arXiv:1912.05399 and "cannot capture the exponential decay" [src: Sect. 2.1, text after Eq. 13]; the classical expansion of the characteristic function is Gaussian at NLO and drops the reflective wall (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 3.1.1, footnote 3]. Check: compute $\Lambda_{0}$ from Eq. 16 or Eq. 23 of arXiv:1912.05399.
- **Confusing the two Fokker–Planck equations.** The forward equation for the field value and the adjoint equation for $\mathcal{N}$ "are conceptually very different objects" (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Sect. 2.1, text before Eq. 12]; Eq. 28 of arXiv:1707.00537 "should in any case not be confused with" the field-value equation [src: Sect. 2.4]. The boundary condition of the first-passage problem is on the starting field value ($P=\delta(\mathcal{N})$ at $\phi_{\rm end}$), not an initial distribution in time.
- **Wrong time variable or noise interpretation.** A time other than N labels "a physically different process" (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 2]; Itô against Stratonovich terms exceed the accuracy of the leading approximation [src: Sect. 2, footnote 5]. Beyond slow roll the noise must be computed in the uniform-N gauge (arXiv:1912.05399) [src: Sect. 2.1, text after Eq. 6], a correction found "negligible in practice" in the cases of arXiv:1905.06300 [src: abstract].
- **Coarse-grained ζ against a Fourier mode.** $\zeta_{\rm cg}$ integrates all modes from $k_{*}$ to $k_{\rm end}$ (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Eq. 13]. For a fixed comoving volume, "In the Gaussian approximation that all these realizations are independent", the variance after its horizon exit is suppressed by "a "central limit theorem" factor $(k/aH)^{3}\sim\mathrm{e}^{{-3N_{k}}}$"; Figueroa et al. "found that in their numerical studies, averaging over kicks after horizon exit had the same effect as simply switching off the noise altogether" (arXiv:2510.04707) [src: Sect. 5.4]; with a noise amplitude decaying exponentially, "the tail reverts to a Gaussian weight" [src: abstract]. An exponential tail is thus a result for the first-passage problem with undamped noise, not for every smoothing. `[der]`
- **Boundary at infinity.** For an unbounded potential the poles do not form a discrete set and the tail is not a pure exponential (arXiv:2511.21388) [src: Sect. 4.1, footnote 6]; on their flat unbounded potential, "without the friction term" the mean lifetime is infinite "and the forward formalism cannot be applied" (arXiv:2511.21388) [src: Sect. 4.1]. Check: state $\phi_{\rm uv}$ and its type, and show the result independent of it in the range used (arXiv:1707.00537) [src: Sect. 2.1.2, text after Eq. 12].
- **Mean as background.** On a hilltop with crossings, rare stuck trajectories "turn out to dominate the mean first-passage time", and Tomberg argues that "the mean does not properly describe the inflationary background", using the median instead (arXiv:2606.09690) [src: abstract]. The forward definition of ζ relative to the mean lifetime is also what differs from the time-reversed approach (arXiv:2511.21388) [src: Sect. 4.1].
- **Separate universe through transitions.** Fails between attractor and non-attractor phases (arXiv:2509.05124), see `approximations.md#separate-universe-and-stochastic-inflation`.
- **Volume weighting.** The volume-weighted Fokker–Planck variant gives a non-normalisable distribution (arXiv:1506.04732) [src: Sect. 2, footnote 6].
- **Printed slips to keep in mind when transcribing.** Eq. 15 of arXiv:1707.00537 lacks the $\mathrm{d}^{2}$ of its numerator (Step 2). In the inline text before Eq. 54 of arXiv:1707.00537, $F^{\prime}(0)$ is printed with $\vartheta_{4}^{\prime\prime}(0,e^{-\mu^{2}/4})$, while Eq. 54 itself carries $e^{-\mu^{2}/\mathcal{N}}$ [src: Sect. 4.1, text before Eq. 54]. Eqs. 12 and 15 of arXiv:2510.04707 print the drift as $V'/(3H)$ against $V'/(3H^{2})$ in their Eq. 7 (Variants). None of the three has a published correction in the versions read.
