## Conventions
<!-- key: conventions -->

- **Harmonic index.** Two conventions for the power per harmonic are in use. The review writes the power of harmonic n as an inverse power of n with exponent q + 1, and q ≈ 1/3 for a loop with a cusp (arXiv:1801.04268) [src: Sect. 9.2, text after Eq. 380] (`formalism.md#topological-defects`). Auclair et al. 2019 and NANOGrav use the exponent q itself, $P_{k}=\frac{\Gamma}{\zeta\left(q\right)}\frac{1}{k^{q}}\,,$ (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Eq. 41], with q = 4/3 for cusps and 5/3 for kinks [src: Sect. V.4, text after Eq. 41]. A "q" quoted without its convention is ambiguous. <!-- num: not a value -->
- **Loop size at birth.** In the one-scale model loops are born at $l=\alpha_{L}L$, a fraction of the long-string correlation length; in the radiation era "the choice $l=\alpha_{L}L$ is equivalent to assuming that $l=\alpha t$, with $\alpha=\alpha_{L}\xi_{r}$" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.1, inline, footnote 6]. Simulation papers quote the cutoff in units of t (arXiv:1309.6637) [src: Sect. IV].
- **Harmonic frequency.** A loop of length ℓ radiates at $f=2k/\ell$ (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4, inline, text after Eq. 40]; Auclair et al. write $\omega_{n}=2\pi n/T$ with $T=l/2$ (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 4.1, inline, text before Eq. 42]. <!-- num: not a value -->
- **Time and number density.** Cosmic time t, and $\mathsf{n}(l,t)$ the number of loops per unit length and volume; scaling variables $x\equiv l/t$ and $\xi\equiv L/t$ (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.1]. Blanco-Pillado, Olum & Shlaer write their density in a scaling variable α and divide by $a^3(t)$ in physical units (arXiv:1309.6637) [src: Sect. IV, Eqs. 62–63]; their α is not the α of the one-scale model. The skill's conventions block (`formalism.md#conventions`) is not used by these papers. <!-- num: not a value -->

## The derivation
<!-- key: the-derivation -->

### Step 1 — the background as a sum over loops
<!-- key: step-1-the-background-as-a-sum-over-loops -->

Auclair et al. (arXiv:1909.00819, Auclair et al. 2019) write the power of a loop as $P_{\rm gw}(f,l)=G\mu^{2}l\penalty\ P(fl),$ [src: Eq. 3]; "Method I" assumes an averaged $P(fl)$ "computed from an ensemble of loops of length $l$ obtained from simulations", and integrates the emission over cosmic time and loop sizes, $\frac{d\rho_{\rm gw}}{df}(t_{0},f)=G\mu^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}{dt\left(\frac{a(t)}{a_{0}}\right)^{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}{dl\penalty\ l\penalty\ \mathsf{n}(l,t)\penalty\ P\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a(t)}fl\right)}}\,,$ (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Eq. 4], the same structure as Eq. 383 of the review (`formalism.md#topological-defects`). The two inputs are the loop number density $\mathsf{n}(l,t)$ (Steps 2–4) and the averaged power spectrum (Step 5). In harmonics, NANOGrav write the same structure as $\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm GW}^{\rm cs}\left(f\right)=\frac{8\pi}{3H_{0}^{2}}\left(G\mu\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm max}}P_{k}\,\mathcal{I}_{k}\left(f\right)\,.$ (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Eq. 40], with $\mathcal{I}_{k}\left(f\right)=\frac{2k}{f}\int_{t_{\rm ini}}^{t_{0}}\textrm{d}t\left(\frac{a\left(t\right)}{a(t_{0})}\right)^{5}n_{l}\left(\frac{2k}{f}\frac{a\left(t\right)}{a(t_{0})},t\right)\,.$ [src: Eq. 42], summing up to $k_{\rm max}=10^{5}$, "which ensures good convergence" (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4, text after Eq. 40].

"Method II" superposes bursts instead: $\frac{d\rho_{\rm gw}}{df}(t_{0},f)=f^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\int_{0}^{\infty}dl\,h^{2}(f,z,l)\,\frac{d^{2}R(z,l)}{dzdl}\,,$ [src: Eq. 5], with the event rate "directly proportional to $\mathsf{n}(l,t)$" [src: Sect. 2.2]; it rests on the high-frequency limit $fl\gg 1$, where "the gravitational waveform sourced by a loop is dominated at high frequency by cusps, kinks, and kink-kink collisions" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 2.2]. Zhang et al. show that the two "provide discrete and continuous representations of the same underlying radiation process and converge on the scale-invariant spectrum plateau", while on the rising low-frequency branch "the two formalisms ... diverge" (arXiv:2610.02590, Zhang et al. 2026) [src: abstract].

### Step 2 — the long strings: velocity-dependent one-scale model
<!-- key: step-2-the-long-strings-velocity-dependent-one -->

The VOS model of Martins & Shellard averages the Nambu–Goto equations over a network of correlation length L, with $\rho_\infty=\mu/L^2$ and a "loop chopping efficiency" $\left(\frac{d\rho_{\infty}}{dt}\right)_{\rm to\ loops}={\tilde{c}}v_{\infty}\frac{\rho_{\infty}}{L}\,.$ (arXiv:hep-ph/0003298, Martins & Shellard 2000) [src: Eq. 2]. The evolution equations are $2\frac{dL}{dt}=2HL(1+{v_{\infty}^{2}})+{{L}\over\ell_{\rm f}}{v_{\infty}^{2}}+{\tilde{c}}v_{\infty}\,,$ [src: Eq. 3] and $\frac{dv_{\infty}}{dt}=\left(1-{v^{2}_{\infty}}\right)\left[\frac{k}{L}-\left(2H+{1\over\ell_{\rm f}}\right)v_{\infty}\right]\,,$ [src: Eq. 5], with $\ell_{\rm f}$ a friction length that "usually becomes irrelevant" after a time $t_{*}\approx(G\mu)^{-1}t_{c}$ [src: Sect. II.1], and k a momentum parameter "related to small-scale structure" with the relativistic ansatz $k_{\rm rel}(v)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\;\frac{1-8v^{6}}{1+8v^{6}}\,.$ (arXiv:hep-ph/0003298) [src: Eq. 7]. Comparisons with simulations "confirm the constancy of the only free parameter", fixed at $\tilde{c}=0.23\pm 0.04\,.$ (arXiv:hep-ph/0003298) [src: Eq. 8].

Auclair et al. use a velocity-dependent ansatz that includes non-relativistic factors, $k({\bar{v}})=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\left(1-{\bar{v}}^{2}\right)\left(1+2\sqrt{2}{\bar{v}}^{3}\right)\frac{1-8{\bar{v}}^{6}}{1+8{\bar{v}}^{6}}\,,$ [src: Eq. 21], "proposed in [120]". For $a\propto t^{\nu}$ with $0<\nu<1$ the attractor is $\frac{L}{t}=\sqrt{\frac{k({\bar{v}})(k({\bar{v}})+{\tilde{c}})}{4\nu(1-\nu)}}\equiv\xi_{s}\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad{\bar{v}}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{k({\bar{v}})}{k({\bar{v}})+{\tilde{c}}}\right)\left(\frac{1-\nu}{\nu}\right)}\equiv{\bar{v}}_{s}\,,$ [src: Eq. 22], with "$\xi_{r}=0.271$ and $v_{r}=0.662$" in radiation and "$\xi_{m}=0.625$ and $v_{m}=0.583$" in matter (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.1, inline, text after Eq. 26 and Eq. 27]. The VOS equations "were shown to provide an accurate description of the long string network evolution in both Nambu-Goto [121] and Abelian-Higgs [122] simulations", while small-scale structure is treated "only ... phenomenologically" [src: Sect. 3.1, text after Eq. 22]. Long-string dynamics beyond this belongs to `thcosmo-pbh-defects` (planned).

### Step 3 — from long strings to loops: the one-scale loop density (Model I)
<!-- key: step-3-from-long-strings-to-loops-the-one-scale -->

Energy balance fixes the normalisation of the loop production function f(x): $\int_{0}^{\infty}{xf(x)\penalty\ dx}=\frac{2}{\xi^{2}}\left[1-\nu(1+{\bar{v}}^{2})\right]={\tilde{c}}\frac{{\bar{v}}}{\xi^{3}}\,.$ [src: Eq. 23]. The model assumes "all loops are assumed to be created with a length $l$ that is a fixed fraction of the characteristic length of the long string network", and adds two corrections, a factor $f_{r}\sim\sqrt{2}$ for the energy that goes into redshifting loop velocities and a factor $\mathcal F$ of "$\mathcal{O}(0.1)$ for Nambu-Goto strings" for the spread of loop sizes, which "generally leads to a decrease of the amplitude of the SGWB" [src: Sect. 3.1, text after Eq. 25]: $f(x)=\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}}{f_{r}}\right){\tilde{C}}\delta\left(x-\alpha_{L}\xi\right)\equiv A\penalty\ \delta\left(x-\alpha_{L}\xi\right)\,.$ [src: Eq. 26]. Deep in the radiation era this gives $\mathsf{n}_{r}(x)=\frac{A_{r}}{\alpha}\frac{\left(\alpha+\mathrm{\Gamma}G\mu\right)^{3/2}}{\left(x+\mathrm{\Gamma}G\mu\right)^{5/2}}\,,$ [src: Eq. 27] with "$A_{r}=0.54$ (we fix ${\cal F}=0.1$, $f_{r}=\sqrt{2}$)", valid for $x\leq\alpha$ [src: Sect. 3.1, text after Eq. 27], and in a matter-only universe $\mathsf{n}_{m}(x)=\frac{A_{m}}{\alpha_{m}}\frac{\alpha_{m}+\mathrm{\Gamma}G\mu}{\left(x+\mathrm{\Gamma}G\mu\right)^{2}}\,,$ with "$A_{m}=0.039$" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Eq. 28 and text after]. NANOGrav use this VOS-based density with "$\mathcal{F}=0.1$ is an efficiency factor" (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4, inline, text after Eq. 43]. <!-- num: not a value -->

### Step 4 — loop densities from Nambu–Goto simulations (BOS and LRS)
<!-- key: step-4-loop-densities-from-nambu-goto -->

**BOS (Model II).** Blanco-Pillado, Olum & Shlaer extract the loop production function from simulations and integrate it, finding in the radiation era $\displaystyle n_{r}(\alpha)\approx\frac{0.52}{\left(\alpha+\Gamma G\mu/2\right)^{5/2}},$ (arXiv:1309.6637, Blanco-Pillado et al. 2013) [src: Eq. 62] "up to a cutoff at $\alpha\approx 0.05$", or in physical units $\displaystyle\frac{n_{r}(t,l)}{a^{3}(t)}\approx\frac{0.18}{t^{3/2}(l+\Gamma G\mu t)^{5/2}},$ [src: Eq. 63] "up to a cutoff at $l\approx 0.1t$"; the functional form agrees with the textbook result "it differs by a significant numerical factor" [src: Sect. IV, text after Eq. 63]. In the matter era, $\displaystyle\frac{n_{m}(t,l)}{a^{3}(t)}\approx\frac{0.27-0.45(l/t)^{0.31}}{t^{2}(l+\Gamma G\mu t)^{2}},$ (arXiv:1309.6637) [src: Eq. 65] "for $l<0.18t$", while "Most importantly in the matter era, there will be loops remaining from the radiation era" [src: Sect. IV, text after Eq. 65]. They state that uncertainties in the late-time loop production function "make no significant contribution to the loop number density" [src: Sect. IV]. Fixing α = 0.1 in the one-scale Eq. 27 gives a numerator of "0.17", "in good agreement" with the BOS 0.18, and "the precise form of the loop production function was argued not to be important" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.2.1].

**LRS (Model III).** Lorenz, Ringeval & Sakellariadou instead take the distribution of non-self-intersecting scaling loops measured in the simulation of Ringeval et al. (arXiv:astro-ph/0511646), $\mathsf{n}(x)=\frac{C_{0}}{x^{p}}\qquad\mathrm{for}\qquad x\gg\mathrm{\Gamma}G\mu\,,$ [src: Eq. 33], with $\left.p=2.60^{+0.21}_{-0.15}\right|_{\mathrm{r}}\,,$ in radiation and $\left.p=2.41^{+0.08}_{-0.07}\right|_{\mathrm{m}}\,,$ in matter [src: Eq. 34], and extend it below the gravitational scale with a Polchinski–Rocha production function $f(x)\propto x^{2\chi-3}$ "for $x>x_{\rm c}$", where "$x_{\rm c}\ll\mathrm{\Gamma}G\mu$ is a scale characteristic of gravitational backreaction" [src: Sect. 3.3, text before Eq. 36], and a backreaction scale $x_{c}\equiv 20(G\mu)^{1+2\chi}.$ [src: Eq. 36], with $\chi_{\mathrm{r}}=0.200^{+0.07}_{-0.10}\,,\qquad\chi_{\mathrm{m}}=0.295^{+0.03}_{-0.04}\,.$ [src: Eq. 37]. The result has "many more small loops" than BOS, which "dominate the stochastic GW spectrum at high frequencies", and the energy balance with the long strings "remains to be fully understood" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.3, text after Eq. 41]. The LRS paper itself states that the density below the gravitational cutoff is "scale independent, proportional to a negative power of the string tension and insensitive to the details of the backreaction modelling" (arXiv:1006.0931, Lorenz et al. 2010) [src: abstract].

**The disagreement.** Blanco-Pillado & Olum measure the loop distribution directly, in volumes "300-2400 times larger" than Ringeval et al., and find exponents "−2.5 in the radiation era and −2 in the matter era"; at the times reached by Ringeval et al. they agree with "the more negative exponents they found, −2.6 and −2.4", but "this was a transient regime", which they take as "further evidence against models with a rapid divergence of the loop density at small scales, such as 'model 3'" (arXiv:1912.10017, Blanco-Pillado & Olum 2019) [src: abstract]. <!-- num: not a value -->

### Step 5 — emission per loop: harmonics, cusps, kinks
<!-- key: step-5-emission-per-loop-harmonics-cusps-kinks -->

In flat space a loop is periodic and radiates at discrete harmonics, $\frac{dP_{n}}{d\mathrm{\Omega}}=8\pi G\mu^{2}n^{2}\left(|A_{+}|^{2}+|A_{\times}|^{2}\right)\,,$ [src: Eq. 42]; for large n, $P_n$ "scales as $n^{-4/3}$, $n^{-5/3}$, and $n^{-2}$" for cusps, kinks and kink–kink collisions, and the emission is "quite anisotropic" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 4.1]. Two averages are used:

- **Realistic smoothed loops.** Blanco-Pillado & Olum smooth simulated loops by "Lorentzian convolution as a model of gravitational back reaction" (arXiv:1709.02693, Blanco-Pillado & Olum 2017) [src: abstract]; "for the great majority of loops, Γ ∼ 50", so that "it is a good approximation to use Γ = 50 always", with an average "Γ̄_r = 51.43" for "1060 loops obtained in 3 radiation era runs" (arXiv:1709.02693) [src: Sect. VI.1]. Auclair et al. add that "there is still some uncertainty", because the smoothing "only shares some of the key ingredients" of later backreaction studies [src: arXiv:1909.00819, Sect. 4.1].
- **Single-event spectra.** The power law of Eq. 41 of arXiv:2306.16219 (Conventions), normalised by ζ(q) so that the harmonics sum to Γ; NANOGrav's numerical spectra deviate from the cusp law below $k\sim 100$, "reaching q values of up to $q\sim 1.5$ around $k\sim 10$" (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4, inline, text after Eq. 41]. Auclair et al. warn that the power law "may not be a good approximation at low harmonics, where the structure of the entire loop becomes important" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 4.1, footnote 10].

For bursts, the logarithmic Fourier transform of the waveform is $h(l,z,f)=A_{q}(l,z)f^{-q}\,,$ [src: Eq. 47], beamed within $\theta_{\rm cutoff}(l,z,f)=\left(\frac{1}{g_{2}f(1+z)l}\right)^{1/3}\,,$ (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Eq. 49], from the calculations of Damour & Vilenkin and others [src: Sect. 4.2]; the numbers of cusps and kinks per oscillation are not known, "it is common to take" both of order one [src: Sect. 4.2, text after Eq. 52]. Damour & Vilenkin stress that this beaming makes the ensemble "strongly non Gaussian", so that the "usually considered 'rms GW background'" is, for large tensions, "an overestimate of the more relevant confusion GW noise because it includes rare, intense bursts" (arXiv:gr-qc/0104026, Damour & Vilenkin 2001) [src: abstract]. Zhang et al. make $N_c$ and $N_k$ free parameters, $\Gamma=\Gamma_{c}(N_{c})+\Gamma_{k}(N_{k})+\Gamma_{kk}(N_{kk}),\qquad N_{kk}\simeq\frac{N_{k}^{2}}{4},$ (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: Eq. 7]; the relation $N_{kk}=N_{k}^{2}/4$ holds "on assuming that there are equal numbers of left- and right-going kinks" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 4.2], an assumption arXiv:2610.02590 does not restate at Eq. 7. In Zhang et al., varying $N_{s}$ "alters the loop decay scale $\Gamma G\mu$" and thus the loop distribution [src: Sect. "Bayesian inference with loop-radiation uncertainty", text after Eq. 7].

Backreaction modifies the cusp burst. In what the authors call "the first continuous numerical evolution of loops under this backreaction", "locally cusps survive backreaction", but the waveform is "significantly modified, weakening the cusp burst and introducing a high-frequency cutoff at $f_c\propto(G\mu)^{-3/2}$" (arXiv:2608.31163, Gerblich et al. 2026) [src: abstract, inline]. Going "beyond the seminal work of Damour and Vilenkin", cusp emission "is not concentrated inside a cone but instead draws a diamond-like shape in the sky", with the maximum "in two slightly offset directions" (arXiv:2610.39983, Auclair et al. 2026) [src: abstract].

### Step 6 — Nambu–Goto against field-theory loops
<!-- key: step-6-nambu-goto-against-field-theory-loops -->

Field-theory strings can decay into "excitations of their elementary constituents". For local strings, simulations of individual oscillating strings and standing waves find massive radiation from "high curvature regions", and otherwise Nambu–Goto motion; in contrast, "large-scale field theory (FT) simulations of the whole network" find loops that "decay within a Hubble time", a discrepancy "under debate for the last ∼20 years", "but the origin of this radiation is not currently understood" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.4]. Their conclusions for LISA rest on "this fundamental assumption" of Nambu–Goto loops [src: Sect. 3.4].

- **Loops from networks decay fast.** Hindmarsh et al. confirm that network loops "disappear quickly, with lifetime proportional to their initial rest-frame length", with constant "$0.14\pm0.04$", while artificially constructed oscillating loops live "approximately as $\ell_\text{init}^2$"; they "argue that whatever the mechanism behind the network loop decay is, it is non-linear, can only be suppressed by careful tuning of initial conditions, and is much stronger than gravitational radiation", so that "one cannot use the Nambu-Goto model to derive robust constraints on the tension of field theory strings" (arXiv:2103.16248, Hindmarsh et al. 2021) [src: abstract].
- **A parameter for the uncertainty.** They advocate "the fraction $f_\text{NG}$ of Nambu-Goto-like loops surviving to radiate gravitationally", estimate "$f_\text{NG}<0.1$ at 95% confidence level", and state that if the NANOGrav signal is due to strings, "$f_\text{NG}$ must be greater than $10^{-3}$" to respect CMB bounds (arXiv:2103.16248) [src: abstract].
- **A critical length.** Matsunami et al. find particle-radiation decay times "proportional to $L^p$ with $p\approx 2$", against "$L$" for gravitational radiation, so particle emission dominates "for loops smaller than a critical length scale, while gravitational losses dominate for larger loops" (arXiv:1903.05102, Matsunami et al. 2019) [src: abstract, inline].
- **Global strings.** For axion-like global strings the GW signal "is suppressed because global strings lose most of their energy by emitting light pseudo-Nambu–Goldstone bosons" (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4]; a 2026 calculation for global networks finds $\Omega_{\rm GW}\propto k$ and $k^{3}$ in the infrared and "a plateau with a mild logarithmic tilt in the ultraviolet" (arXiv:2609.22430, Litos et al. 2026) [src: abstract, inline].

### Step 7 — how the PTA fits depend on these choices
<!-- key: step-7-how-the-pta-fits-depend-on-these-choices -->

- **Emission model, fixed loop density.** NANOGrav fix $\Gamma=50$ "as suggested by numerical simulations", use the VOS-based density of Step 3, and compare four stable-string models: cusps only (q = 4/3), kinks only (q = 5/3), monochromatic emission at $f=2/\ell$, and numerical loop spectra "including GWs from cusps and kinks" (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4, text after Eq. 41]. Against the SMBHB baseline the Bayes factors are "$\mathcal{B}=0.277\pm 0.006$, $\mathcal{B}=0.364\pm 0.008$, $\mathcal{B}=0.379\pm 0.008$, and $\mathcal{B}=0.307\pm 0.006$" respectively, and "stable cosmic strings represent the only case that consistently yields a Bayes factor in favor of the smbhb interpretation" (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4, "Results and discussion"]. The choice of q moves the Bayes factor within this narrow range; it does not change the verdict for stable strings in that analysis `[der]`. <!-- num: not a value -->
- **Loop density, with LVK.** Zhang et al. jointly fit NANOGrav, EPTA and LVK with BOS (Model A), LRS (Model B) and two hybrids (C-1, C-2), sampling $N_c$ and $N_k$ (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: Supplementary Material, Sect. S-III]. For BOS the harmonic calculation gives a joint "$G\mu<1.3\times 10^{-10}$", PTA dominated (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: Sect. "Bayesian inference with loop-radiation uncertainty", inline, text after Eq. 12]; for the other three, LVK imposes $G\mu<\left\{2.0\times 10^{-15},\,1.1\times 10^{-15},\,2.5\times 10^{-15}\right\},$ (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: Eq. 13], so that "only Model A can account for the PTA observation without violating the LVK bound"; even then "the NANOGrav data do not favor a combined background from Model A cosmic strings and SMBHBs over an SMBHB-only explanation", with Bayes factors "1.00 and 0.91 for the harmonic and burst formalisms" (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: Sect. "Bayesian inference with loop-radiation uncertainty", text after Eq. 13].
- **Harmonic against burst formalism.** Using harmonics "strengthens the LVK bounds by 21%−31% for three of these models" (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: abstract].
- **Field-theory loops.** NANOGrav's verdict is stated "with the exception of stable cosmic strings of field theory origin" (`models.md#cosmic-strings-nambu-goto-loops`); with the $f_{\rm NG}$ parametrisation of Step 6 the required fraction is the lower limit quoted there (arXiv:2103.16248) [src: abstract]. Metastable strings and superstrings are separate models (arXiv:2306.16219) [src: Sect. V.4].
- **For LISA (forecast).** BOS and VOS modelling "become distinguishable confidently for $G\mu\gtrsim 5\cdot 10^{-13}$" in a simulation-based reconstruction (forecast, arXiv:2508.05395, Dimitriou et al. 2025) [src: abstract].

## Variants and what they cost
<!-- key: variants-and-what-they-cost -->

- **One-scale (Model I).** Analytic, follows the radiation–matter transition through the VOS equations; carries the ad hoc factors $\mathcal F$ <!-- eq: notation --> and $f_r$ <!-- eq: notation --> and a single loop size (Step 3). Multi-scale models "contain a larger number of phenomenological parameters", and the backreaction parameter "cannot be calibrated with simulations" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.1, footnote 5].
- **BOS (Model II).** Simulation-calibrated production function; normalisation close to Model I when the one-scale loop size is set to the simulated cutoff (Step 4).
- **LRS (Model III).** Simulation-calibrated density plus a Polchinski–Rocha small-scale model; many small loops and a high-frequency excess; disputed by direct measurements (arXiv:1912.10017) [src: abstract] and the model most strongly bounded by LVK in a joint analysis (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: Eq. 13].
- **Harmonics or bursts.** Equivalent on the plateau; the burst sum misses the discreteness of the first harmonics on the rising branch (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: abstract].
- **Field theory.** If loops "decay within a Hubble time", they "do not contribute as a source of GWs all through cosmic history" (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 3.4]; the surviving Nambu–Goto-like fraction is then the open parameter (arXiv:2103.16248) [src: abstract].

## What goes wrong
<!-- key: what-goes-wrong -->

- **Mixing harmonic-index conventions.** q ≈ 1/3 in the review and q = 4/3 in Auclair et al. and NANOGrav both denote cusps (see Conventions). Check: the exponent of n in the formula, not the letter. <!-- num: not a value -->
- **Low harmonics from a large-n law.** The single-event power laws are large-n results (Step 5); the rising branch, where PTA sit for small tensions, is where harmonics and bursts differ (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: abstract].
- **Energy budget of small loops.** LRS's small-loop population leaves the energy balance with the long strings open (Step 4); its exponents may be transient (arXiv:1912.10017) [src: abstract].
- **Γ held fixed while the emission model varies.** The loop density depends on $\Gamma G\mu$, so a change of cusp or kink content must be propagated into $\mathsf{n}$; "for consistency it is important" to use the same Γ (arXiv:1909.00819) [src: Sect. 4.1, text after Eq. 46]; Zhang et al. sample this dependence (arXiv:2610.02590) [src: text after Eq. 7].
- **Rms background against confusion noise.** For large tensions the rms background includes rare bursts and overestimates the confusion noise (arXiv:gr-qc/0104026) [src: abstract].
- **Nambu–Goto bounds applied to field-theory strings.** Not robust according to Hindmarsh et al. (arXiv:2103.16248) [src: abstract]; quote $f_{\rm NG}$ or the loop picture with any bound.
- **Printed slips in arXiv:1909.00819 (v1), copied, not corrected.** Sect. 3.1 says the loop density "in general requires solving the VOS equations (19) and (20)", whereas the VOS equations are its Eqs. 18–19 and Eq. 20 is the loop-energy relation [src: Sect. 3.1, text after Eq. 26]; Eq. 35 prints the LRS normalisation as $\left.C_{0}=0.21^{-0.12}_{+0.13}\right|_{\mathrm{r}}$, with the minus sign on the upper error [src: Eq. 35]. No erratum was looked for.
- **A printed slip in arXiv:1709.02693 (v2).** The introduction states that "the gravitational power emitted is ΓGμ", where the emission rate is $\Gamma G\mu^2$ elsewhere (`formalism.md#topological-defects`) [src: Sect. I, inline]; copied, not corrected.
- **Two cutoffs in one paper.** BOS quote the radiation-era cutoff as α ≈ 0.05 in their scaling variable and l ≈ 0.1t in physical units (arXiv:1309.6637) [src: Sect. IV, text after Eqs. 62–63]; the relation between their α and l/t is not stated in the section read. Do not identify their α with the one-scale α.
